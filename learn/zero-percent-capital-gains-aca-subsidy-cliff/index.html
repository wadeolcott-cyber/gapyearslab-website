How 0% Capital Gains Can Still Cost You Over $23,000 in ACA Subsidies

Why early retirees need to consider healthcare costs alongside federal income taxes.

This article addresses 2026 tax and ACA coverage rules. The filing-method comparison uses reviewed draft 2026 Form 8962 instructions, not certified final instructions.

The surprising consequence

A 0% federal tax rate on an investment gain sounds like a straightforward opportunity. But the rate applied to that gain is only part of the picture if your household buys health insurance through the Affordable Care Act (ACA) Marketplace.

Long-term gains can increase ACA household income even when they generate no regular federal income tax. That can change how much help a household receives with its insurance premiums.

How could realizing just one additional dollar of investment gains increase annual healthcare costs by more than $23,000? For the hypothetical household below, the answer is a specific income eligibility threshold. This is not an outcome every early retiree faces.

The 0% capital gains opportunity

After leaving full-time work, people often have more flexibility over when to realize investment gains, withdraw retirement savings, or convert money to a Roth IRA. Lower ordinary income may leave room for eligible long-term gains at a 0% federal rate.

For married couples filing jointly in 2026, the 0% long-term capital gains ceiling is $98,900 in taxable income. Ordinary income and deductions affect how much of that band remains available. Qualified dividends receive similar preferential treatment.

Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends generally still count toward ACA modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). A gain’s tax rate is different from whether that gain counts as household income.

Why the ACA rules changed in 2026

The enhanced premium tax credits available during 2021–2025 expired after 2025. Under the reviewed 2026 rules, the general premium tax credit income eligibility ceiling is again 400% of the applicable federal poverty guideline.

The threshold depends on household size and geography. For 2026 coverage, the relevant guideline is from 2025. For four people in the contiguous United States or Washington, D.C., that guideline is $32,150; 400% is $128,600.

Eligibility also depends on more than income. This example assumes eligible full-year Marketplace coverage and no alternative coverage, allocation, or special eligibility complications.

A one-dollar difference with a $23,191 consequence

Consider a married couple filing jointly with four ACA household members in the contiguous states or D.C. Both scenarios use the same dividends. Only realized long-term capital gains change:

Annual income: one additional dollar of long-term gains
Annual measure Scenario A Scenario B
Nonqualified dividends $27,000 $27,000
Qualified dividends $13,000 $13,000
Long-term capital gains $88,600 $88,601
Total ACA household income $128,600 $128,601

The nonqualified and qualified dividend amounts are separate, disjoint categories here. On Form 1040, line 3b includes total ordinary dividends, including the qualified portion, while line 3a separately identifies the qualified subset. These inputs correspond to $40,000 on line 3b, with $13,000 identified on line 3a—not $53,000 of total dividends. The qualified portion is not added twice.

Assume a selected Marketplace plan costing $2,500 per month, or $30,000 annually, and an applicable benchmark Silver plan costing $3,000 per month, or $36,000 annually. These are hypothetical premiums, not actual Marketplace quotes.

The selected plan premium is the starting cost of the coverage the household chooses. The benchmark helps determine the credit; it is not automatically the cost of that chosen plan.

Annual measure Scenario A Scenario B
ACA household income $128,600 $128,601

Estimated premium tax credit Approximately $23,191 $0
Net selected-plan premium Approximately $6,809 $30,000

One additional dollar of reported income increases annual net healthcare costs by approximately $23,191 in this hypothetical scenario.

Scenario A is exactly at the modeled income threshold. Scenario B is above it and loses the credit under these assumptions. “Healthcare costs” in this comparison means net insurance premiums, not medical bills, deductibles, or all out-of-pocket expenses.

Why federal income tax remains $0

The household has $27,000 in nonqualified dividends, which receive ordinary-income treatment. Qualified dividends and eligible long-term gains receive preferential treatment. The $32,200 base standard deduction for married filing jointly in 2026 absorbs the ordinary income and $5,200 of preferential income.

That leaves $96,400 of taxable preferential income in A and $96,401 in B, both below the $98,900 0% ceiling. The validated income mix therefore produces $0 regular federal income tax before credits in both scenarios.

This does not mean the household owes no federal taxes of any kind. Other taxes and special circumstances may apply and are outside this example.

A note about tax-return rounding

The Income Planner preserves cents and uses continuous mathematical estimates. At Scenario A, it produces $23,191.44 in planning premium tax credit and $6,808.56 in net annual premiums.

Under the reviewed draft 2026 annual-line Form 8962 filing method, the corresponding values are $23,191 and $6,809. The prominent whole-dollar figures above match that reviewed annual filing comparator and the planning amounts rounded for article presentation.

Tax returns may use consistent whole-dollar reporting conventions. Amounts combined on one reporting line are added before rounding the total. A one-cent change in raw income does not necessarily change reported whole-dollar income, and different sources can belong on different lines.

Using a $1 difference here avoids suggesting that an extra cent must eliminate a filed credit. Rounding is not a strategy for bypassing eligibility rules, and the annual and monthly filing paths are not interchangeable choices for maximizing a credit.

The reviewed 2026 Form 8962 instructions were draft, not for filing. Actual reconciliation depends on final IRS instructions, Form 1095-A records, eligibility, and household circumstances. This article does not claim final 2026 return accuracy.

Four practical lessons

  1. Consider taxes and healthcare costs together. A favorable federal income tax result can coexist with higher net premiums.
  2. Distinguish sale proceeds from realized gains. Gross investment-sale proceeds are not the same as the taxable gain after basis and applicable rules.
  3. Coordinate Roth conversions with investment sales. Both can affect annual income and ACA MAGI; compare their combined effects rather than viewing each decision alone.
  4. Allow a reasonable margin for uncertain income. Dividends, year-end distributions, and other changes can affect income. Avoid depending on an exact threshold.

These are educational considerations, not individualized tax or investment recommendations. Realizing gains is not inherently a poor financial decision.

Methodology, assumptions, and sources

This article follows the independently derived whole-dollar comparison in the Income Planner’s merged PR #47 rounding review. It uses the new $128,600/$128,601 reference pair, not the older one-cent example. The application repository is private; this page provides an accessible summary and official source links.

  • Tax and coverage year 2026; married filing jointly; four ACA household members; contiguous states/D.C.; 2025 HHS poverty guidelines for 2026 coverage.
  • Base standard deduction, with no age/blindness additions or itemization. Eligible preferential treatment for the specified qualified dividends and long-term gains; no capital losses or carryforwards.
  • Full-year qualifying Marketplace coverage; selected and applicable benchmark premiums fixed at $2,500 and $3,000 monthly. All selected premiums paid; required eligibility assumptions satisfied; no alternative coverage, special allocations, or other eligibility complications.
  • No other income, relevant adjustments, or MAGI addbacks. Advance premium tax credits explicitly $0 in every month for the validation comparison; advances and final allowable credits are distinct.
  • Federal regular income tax is before credits. State/local, payroll/self-employment, NIIT, AMT, other credits and unsupported tax treatments are excluded. This is not full tax-return preparation or comprehensive insurance eligibility determination.
  • The annual filing comparator uses reviewed draft 2026 Form 8962 instructions dated October 20, 2026. Final 2026 instructions were not located at the report’s checked endpoints. Earlier final 2025 instructions support reporting/worksheet structure only; their enhanced credit percentages are not carried into 2026.

Actual results depend on final IRS guidance and personal circumstances. The hypothetical premiums are not quotes, and the example is not a guarantee of Marketplace eligibility or a universal outcome for early retirees.
