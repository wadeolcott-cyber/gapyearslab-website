The Hidden Cost of Roth Conversions in Early Retirement

Why a $10,000 Roth conversion can increase federal taxes and healthcare costs by more than $2,000—even without crossing the ACA subsidy cliff.

Early retirement can create an opportunity to move money from traditional retirement accounts into Roth IRAs while your taxable income is relatively low.

Roth conversions can be a valuable strategy. You pay income taxes on the taxable amount converted today in exchange for the potential for tax-free qualified withdrawals later.

But if you're purchasing health insurance through the Affordable Care Act (ACA) Marketplace, there's another cost to consider.

A Roth conversion can increase your healthcare costs even when it doesn't push you over the ACA subsidy eligibility threshold.

A $10,000 Roth conversion with a $2,056 cost

Consider a hypothetical married couple filing jointly in 2026, both purchasing health insurance through the ACA Marketplace.

Their household has $65,000 in annual income from part-time employment and investments. They're considering converting $10,000 from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA.

Here's what happens when we model that decision using the GapYears Income Planner:

A $10,000 conversion increases combined estimated annual costs by $2,056.

That increase comes from $1,060 in additional federal income tax and $996 in reduced premium tax credits.

The regular federal income tax increase is 10.6 cents per dollar converted. Once the reduced healthcare assistance is included, the combined increase is 20.6 cents per dollar converted.

These are hypothetical results, not rates that apply to every household.

Why does healthcare cost more?

The ACA offers a premium tax credit (PTC) that can help eligible households pay for Marketplace health insurance.

The credit depends partly on household income. A taxable Roth conversion generally increases that income, potentially reducing the credit.

In this example, the household remains below the 2026 ACA subsidy eligibility threshold of $84,600 for a two-person household in the contiguous United States.

Its credit declines from $15,126 to $14,130, but it doesn't lose eligibility altogether.

Why exactly $996?

For 2026, the applicable household contribution percentage is 9.96% when income falls between 300% and 400% of the federal poverty guideline. Both scenarios fall within that range.

The additional $10,000 of household income therefore reduces the credit by $996 ($10,000 × 9.96%), under the example's assumptions.

You don't have to cross the ACA subsidy cliff for additional income to increase your healthcare costs.

This example is different from the ACA cliff scenario featured on the GapYears Lab homepage. The two examples use different income and insurance assumptions to illustrate different planning risks.

Where does the money come from to cover the additional costs?

There's another consideration that can be easy to overlook.

A Roth conversion doesn't provide cash for everyday expenses. The $10,000 moves from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA, where it can remain invested.

If the entire amount is converted, the household needs another source of funds to cover the additional $2,056 in taxes and insurance costs.

A taxable brokerage account or cash savings can be particularly useful. Additional retirement-account withdrawals or withholding may create more taxable income, reduce premium tax credits, or trigger early-distribution penalties.

When are the additional costs paid?

In this example, the household receives $0 in advance premium tax credits. It pays the full $1,600 monthly insurance premium throughout the year and claims its eligible PTC when filing its federal tax return.

The conversion reduces that tax-time credit by $996, resulting in a smaller refund or a larger balance owed compared with the baseline.

Households using advance premium tax credits may instead experience changes to their monthly out-of-pocket payments if they update their Marketplace income estimates. Advance payments are reconciled against the final allowable credit when filing taxes.

Does that make the Roth conversion a bad decision?

Not necessarily.

Converting traditional retirement savings to Roth can reduce future taxable retirement balances, potentially lower required minimum distributions, and create opportunities for tax-free qualified withdrawals.

Those long-term benefits may outweigh the additional $2,056 in current-year costs.

But they may not.

The answer depends on future tax rates, investment growth, retirement income needs, and other household circumstances.

The important lesson is that your federal income tax bracket alone doesn't tell you the full current-year cost of a Roth conversion.

Converting the largest amount possible within a tax bracket isn't necessarily the best strategy.

Three timing and tax considerations

If you're considering a 2026 Roth conversion, keep these rules in mind:

  • Year-end timing: For a conversion counted in 2026, the traditional IRA distribution generally must occur by December 31, 2026. Custodian processing deadlines and any applicable rollover-completion rules also matter.
  • The five-year rule: If you're younger than 59½, withdrawing taxable converted amounts within the separate five-year period applicable to that conversion may trigger a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.
  • After-tax IRA contributions: If you have nondeductible traditional IRA contributions, the taxable portion of your conversion generally must account for your IRA basis across applicable traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances under the pro-rata rules.

See IRS Publication 590-B and Form 8606 instructions for the applicable rules.

The bottom line

Roth conversions can be valuable during early retirement, particularly when taxable income is relatively low.

But the full current-year cost may include income taxes, reduced healthcare assistance, and the need for accessible savings to cover those expenses.

A good Roth conversion strategy isn't simply about paying less tax today. It's about understanding what the conversion costs now, how you'll cover those costs, and what it might save in the future.

Example assumptions and limitations

This hypothetical 2026 example assumes married filing jointly, a two-person ACA household in the contiguous United States, full-year eligible Marketplace coverage, and $0 in advance premium tax credits for all 12 months.

Baseline annual income consists of $40,000 in wages, $5,000 in taxable interest, $5,000 in nonqualified dividends, and $15,000 in long-term capital gains. The modified scenario adds a fully taxable $10,000 Roth conversion.

Monthly selected-plan premiums are $1,600, and applicable benchmark Silver premiums are $1,800. The example applies the 2026 federal standard deduction and ACA contribution rules.

The annual premiums after PTC represent the economic cost of insurance after applying the final allowable credit—not the monthly cash payments in this example.

Federal income tax is shown before credits. State and payroll taxes, other potential taxes, and lifetime conversion benefits are not included. Results depend on household circumstances and eligibility.

These figures represent two different scenarios within the same hypothetical household. They are not intended to reproduce the separate Roth conversion illustration featured on the homepage.
